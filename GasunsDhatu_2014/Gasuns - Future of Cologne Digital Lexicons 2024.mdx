**Future of Cologne Digital Lexicons**

> **Mārcis Gasūns**
>
> Sanskrit Zealots's Society / Russia, Obninsk
>
> gasyoun@gmail.com

**Abstract**

> In the process of direct observation, we only have access to speech, which is the outcome of language use. Linguists can be compared to hunters, paleozoologists, or medical diagnosticians, reconstructing events or appearances by analyzing the traces and manifestations left behind. Y.D. Apresyan suggests an \"integral description of a language\" where grammar and vocabulary are interconnected and mutually influenced. The set of grammar and vocabulary proposed by Apresyan forms a unity where grammatical rules take into account the constraints imposed by the dictionary properties. This is similar to the Dhatupatha and Ganapatha lists that are referred in Paninian grammatical rules. But never before we have tried to connect grammar, corpus and dictionary together in the digital space so wide and deep at the same time. The time has come. And we are able to demonstrate how it works in the field of Sanskrit digital studies.
>
> In direct observation, language is presented as a reconstructed vocabulary and grammar, reflecting the perceptions of linguists, which are shaped by both theoretical views and the amount of language data available. Since texts reflect language and dictionaries reflect texts, the lexical-semantic system of a language in a dictionary can be seen as a \"reflection of reflection\".
>
> Continuing the lexicographical discussion that commenced in October 2019 at Kharagpur with the \"History of Cologne Digital Lexicons\" at the previous ISCLS session, I would like to present the \"Future of Cologne Digital Lexicons\". It has been 10 years since the development of the Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries moved to <https://github.com/sanskrit-lexicon/> and became as open as any Sanskrit project has ever been before.
>
> Every single digital library does not equal to a corpus, so we will talk also about the issues connected with the development of biggest Sanskrit parallel text corpus, the Sanskit-Russian-English-German <https://samskrtam.ru/parallel-corpus/> that has become the first and remains the main source for cross-reference links in the digital dictionaries at Cologne, linking dictionary back to corpora.

The mind-boggling changes which lexicography (and the computer-world at large) underwent in just half a century are dramatic. From pre-personal computers with data punched into cards and paper tape, to today's software in the cloud which independently prepares draft corpus-driven dictionary entries for lexicographers to review though remains a far future for Indian languages, but has become a reality for users to contribute to via crowdsourcing. And it is the crowdsourcing which we would want to speak about from the perspective of planning for the next half a century in Sanskrit lexicography.

In what ways are digital dictionaries already able to serve users better than hard copy dictionaries? The use of large corpora during dictionary compilation, combined with software which helps to analyse and synthesise the huge amounts of data. From the perspective of the dictionary maker, this ensures being certain that nothing important has been overlooked; or even: that what is frequent and common is effectively covered in the dictionary, while knowing that one has safely omitted what is truly less important.

"The first \[dictionary\] to use computers extensively in its editorial preparation" was Random House Unabridged. We in the field of Sanskrit lexicography, have not yet used computers extensively in editorial preparation. For many years since 2007 the existing Sanskrit e-dictionaries were merely printed dictionaries made available in an electronic platform. And we are still bad in making them even a 1 to 1 copy, because data entry errors, encoding of samāsas and dictionary structure markup is nowhere even close to perfect even for the most widely used Monier Williams Sanskrit-English dictionary, 1899 edition. Tens of thousands of mistakes or markup issues that can't be fixed in a single decade.

In 2024 we are still living by mid-19 century dictionary standards in the field of Indian lexicography. Corpus revolution in lexicography has not yet reached the field of Sanskrit and Cologne Digital Lexicons will be the frontier, when it will.

After 30 years in business, Cologne remains the origin of Sanskrit digital dictionaries, the industry standard. From the 42 Digital Lexicons at Cologne, 8 dictionaries are chosen for highest priority:

• WIL 1832 Wilson Sanskrit-English Dictionary

• MW 1899 Monier-Williams Sanskrit-English Dictionary

• AP 1957 Practical Sanskrit-English Dictionary

• PWG 1855 Böhtlingk and Roth Grosses Petersburger Wörterbuch

• PW 1879 Böhtlingk Sanskrit-Wörterbuch in KürzererFassung

• SCH 1928 Schmidt Nachträge zum Sanskrit-Wörterbuch+ Nachträge und Verbesserungen

• VCP \* 1873 Vacaspatyam

• SKD \* 1886 Sabda-kalpadruma

What started as a project led by Thomas Malten (originally from Germany, now in Cambodia) in Cologne, multiplied by Peter Scharf (then USA, now in India) and coded by Malcolm D. Hyman (Germany), is now under the full leadership of Jim Funderburk (USA). Since then, volunteers have joined, including Dr. Sampada Savardeka, and more sporadically, Felix Rau and Jonathan Migliori.

Since 2007, Dr. Mārcis Gasūns (Russia) has been communicating via emails and in 2014 he founded the GitHub repository. He was joined by new team members in: Dr. Dhaval Patel, Dr. Usha Sanka, Nāgabhūṣaṇarāvu Kālepu (India) and Anna Rybakova (Russia).

The issues quickly piled up between 2014 and 2024 were not so quick to be solved. We have been made aware of hundreds and thousands of issues related to the eight main dictionaries, not to mention the dozens of others. As the foremost authoritative resource for Sanskrit dictionaries, there is a pressing need to expand the team of volunteers dedicated to Sanskrit lexicography on a monthly basis.

If we are to make a two-century progress in the field of Sanskrit lexicography, an evolution will not help, only a revolution can. The worst one can do when compiling dictionaries nowadays is to use introspection only. We need data (as found in corpora), and a linguistic theory to analyse and synthesise that data.

In 2024 the beginning of addition of 5 Sanskrit-Russian Dictionaries has been planned at Cologne, additionally to the already available Sanskrit-English, English-Sanskrit, Sanskrit-French, Sanskrit-German, Sanskrit-Latin Dictionaries. They have to be modified to comply with the DTD of XML at Cologne. The DTD or a schema is kept separate from both the unique article-specific data on the one hand and the repetitive metadata on the other, so truly instantaneous tailoring is effectively made possible for digital dictionaries.

A demo of an offline Russia-born Sanskrit search engine "The Churning of the Ocean", that is based on the open corpora, will be demonstrated. Once the Digital Clay Sanskrit Library in 2006 planned for such search features, never went open and was closed even before finalized, when the funding went over. So, the German-based Cologne applied linguistics experience and artificial intelligence experiments, together with Russian and Indian students crowdsourcing could become the safe and clean island of verified Sanskrit text data-set, that could become the new universal standard worldwide.
